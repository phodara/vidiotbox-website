# Philosophy of Haiku

Working notes for the [Vidiotbox cosmic haiku collection](../cosmic-haikus.html).

Our aim is to make cosmic scale, mystery, and spiritual realization felt through a few precise words. A successful poem gives the reader something to experience and room to discover its meaning.

These notes preserve our discussion of the collection and offer a direction for revision. They describe an editorial preference for this project, not a universal rule for every haiku tradition.

## Form and feeling

We have chosen a 5–7–5 syllable pattern for this collection. That constraint can encourage concentration, but correct counting alone does not produce the feeling of haiku. A poem can satisfy the pattern while sounding like an explanation, a philosophical statement, or a sentence divided into three lines.

The [Haiku Society of America’s definition](https://hsa-haiku.org/hsa-definitions.html) emphasizes imagery, an experience of nature or season, and a meaningful relationship between images, perceptions, or ideas. This contemporary English-language definition is a useful reference for our discussion; it does not exhaust the history or possibilities of haiku.

For this project, the qualities we want to develop are precise perception, natural language, a meaningful shift within the poem, and an ending that leaves the reader room to respond. Seasonal details can help establish a particular moment when they belong naturally in the scene.

The original AI drafts placed too much emphasis on meeting the syllable count. Future revisions should give equal attention to clarity, imagery, and the relationship between the three lines.

## What the current poems reveal

Some of the observational space poems feel close to traditional haiku. Much of the Singularity collection feels more like philosophical meditation arranged in 5–7–5. These approaches can both produce worthwhile poems, but they create different reading experiences.

### Moonset: a scene that carries the feeling

> The moon meets the ridge  
> And slips behind the black pines  
> Frost stays on the gate

This poem has a specific scene, a seasonal detail, and a quiet shift from the moving moon to the still frost. It brings distant movement and nearby stillness into the same moment. The reader can discover a feeling of transience or continuity without being told what conclusion to draw.

Of these examples, this has the strongest traditional haiku feel.

### Humility: a realization stated directly

> Before such vastness  
> I need not stand at the heart  
> Of all that exists

This poem expresses a clear philosophical realization: the speaker does not need to occupy the center of existence. It invites reflection on that idea, but offers little sensory experience through which the reader can discover it independently.

Its strength is directness. Its character is closer to a short meditation than to the observed moment in “Moonset.” Calling it philosophical does not make it a failed poem; it identifies what the poem is doing.

### Hollow Bell: spiritual feeling through sensation

> I strike the bronze bell  
> Its note thins against the dark  
> My ribs hold the hum

Here, physical action becomes sound, then bodily sensation. The fading note and the continuing vibration give the poem a perceptible movement. Its spiritual resonance can emerge from the experience without a statement about enlightenment, infinity, or the soul.

Within the Singularity collection, this comes closer to the haiku feeling we want to develop. Its connection to singularity remains indirect; a strong sensory poem still needs a meaningful place within the collection’s cosmic theme.

## Cosmic subjects and spiritual realization

A cosmic subject can work through an encounter: light through a telescope, a star disappearing behind a ridge, a hand covering part of the sky, or an instrument reaching the limit of what it can measure. Such details can give the reader access to questions too large to picture directly.

Singularity is especially difficult because its scale and implications resist ordinary intuition. An imagined scene can still be precise and coherent. The poem should make its relationships intelligible even when the subject remains mysterious.

Words such as “mind,” “vastness,” “nothingness,” and “soul” are available to us. When too many poems depend on them, however, the collection can become repetitive and explanatory. Each abstract word should earn its place through what the poem actually makes us perceive or understand.

Spiritual realization may be explicit when directness is the purpose. When seeking a more traditional haiku feel, we should let it arise through attention, a change of perspective, or the relationship between images.

Ordinary objects also need a reason to appear. Tea, rice, bread, or a household task does not automatically give a poem depth. An everyday ending should deepen the cosmic subject or create an understandable connection. It should not feel attached merely to make an abstract poem seem grounded.

## Principles for revision

1. **Find the experience.** Identify what the reader can see, hear, touch, or imagine clearly. If the poem is deliberately conceptual, identify its precise idea.
2. **Make the relationship clear.** The images may surprise us, but their connection should reward attention. Obscurity alone does not create mystery.
3. **Give the ending a purpose.** The final line can change the scale, introduce another perception, or deepen the scene. Check whether it merely explains what the first two lines already suggest.
4. **Keep language natural.** Avoid padding, strained word order, and vague substitutions made only to satisfy the count.
5. **Preserve the cosmic connection.** A concrete image should contribute to the subject. A poem about singularity should retain some meaningful relationship to singularity, even when that relationship is indirect.
6. **Let the reader participate.** Consider whether the poem leaves space for discovery or supplies every interpretation in advance.
7. **Check the form after the meaning works.** Read aloud, verify grammar, and count 5–7–5. Notice pronunciation-dependent words such as “toward” and “our.” Revise the line as a whole when a mechanical fix weakens it.

The collection can include both sensory haiku and more explicit philosophical meditations. Revision should make each poem’s approach deliberate, while keeping the language clear enough for a reader to enter it.
