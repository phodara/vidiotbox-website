Building with AI.
Questioning
what it means.

After more than thirty years of developing software and hardware, I’m exploring how AI changes the way we build, create, and understand our own intelligence.

The starting question

When a machine helps us make something, what exactly have we created—and what have we come to understand?

Four projects. An open inquiry.

Hardware, software, and creative experiments.

01 / Tools & development

Pocket Prowler

A handheld Wi-Fi and Bluetooth scanner, bringing firmware, electronics, and a custom enclosure into one practical tool.

How does AI change the work of turning an idea into something that works?

02 / Expression & perception

Vidiotbox

A playful meeting of hardware, animated faces, and digital expression. A place to experiment with how machines appear to have character.

What do we bring to a machine when we see a personality in it?

03 / Language & authorship

hAIku-matic

A small AI language model writing haiku, in a browser or on a pocket robot poet. Human choices shape the system; the system generates the words.

Where does creativity reside: in the maker, the model, the reader, or their encounter?

04 / Symbols & culture

‘Do Not’ Pictograms

We have traveled a lot internationally, and I have always been fascinated with international signage. How do we create non-verbal messaging that doesn’t depend on cultural attributes? Does it always work?

Why are most of these messages warnings about what not to do?

Lines of inquiry

On creativity & authorship

When a machine helps us create, what makes the work ours?

Where do intention, originality, and authorship reside when people and machines work together? Who or what is the creator and who or what is the tool?

On intelligence & consciousness

When we sense a mind behind the machine, what do we see?

An expression can suggest emotion; words can suggest understanding. What evidence would distinguish our interpretation from a machine’s subjective experience?

On responsibility & human agency

As our tools become more capable, what do we owe?

What responsibilities come with the power to create? Questions of credit, consent, resources, and consequences belong inside the experiment, from the beginning.

On efficiency & understanding

When we can do more, faster, what do we understand?

Does speed create room for deeper thought, or let us bypass it? What happens to craftsmanship, learning, and judgment when more of the work is delegated?

A personal statement

Making things.
Examining the consequences.

Paul Hodara
A working philosophy, open to revision.

As an artist and technologist with more than thirty years of experience developing software and hardware, I have watched new tools reshape both what we can make and how we think. I have anticipated the arrival of increasingly capable artificial intelligence with curiosity and caution. This website is my space for engaging with this newest tool through direct experience: building, experimenting, and asking what the results reveal.

Pocket Prowler, Vidiotbox, hAIku-matic, and the ‘Do Not’ Pictograms are different expressions of my inquiry. They connect practical development, playful digital expression, machine-generated poetry, and cultural commentary. Through them, I am exploring AI’s role in the development process while designing projects that challenge our assumptions about intelligence, creativity, and discovery.

The ‘Do Not’ Pictograms use humor to explore how we communicate rules through images. They ask whether symbols can cross cultural boundaries, what assumptions we bring to them, and why so much public signage tells us what not to do.

For me, building with AI means taking its possibilities seriously—and taking the questions it raises just as seriously. For example:

What does efficiency actually give us?

AI invites us to reconsider the time and expertise required to develop an idea. But speed alone is an incomplete measure of progress. Does faster development deepen our understanding—or allow us to bypass it? What happens to craftsmanship, judgment, and learning when more of the work is delegated? I want to explore what we gain, what we risk losing, and how we remain responsible for what we build.

Who creates, and who is responsible?

When a system generates a poem or helps shape a design, how should we understand originality, intention, and authorship? Whose work made the output possible, and how should credit, consent, and compensation be addressed? The pleasure of making something does not remove the obligation to consider its origins, its effects on other people, and the resources it consumes.

What do we mean by intelligence and consciousness?

Words matter. Intelligence, creativity, and consciousness carry different meanings for different people. Before drawing conclusions, we need to explain what we mean and what evidence supports our claims. An expressive face can invite us to perceive emotion; a compelling poem can invite us to imagine a mind behind it. What do these responses tell us about the machine—and about ourselves? Does convincing behavior imply understanding or subjective experience? How would we know?

Knowing how a language model works does not fully explain why it gives a particular response. I want to explore its abilities without mistaking humanlike behavior for human experience.

I do not offer these projects as answers. They are opportunities to make the questions tangible, share what I am learning, and invite discussion. As AI becomes more capable, questions of moral responsibility, human agency, and meaningful control become more urgent. We should confront them while our choices can still shape what follows.
